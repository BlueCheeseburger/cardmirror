## Round Structure


| Speech | HS Time | Notes                                                                                               |
| ------ | ------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1AC    | 8 min   | Aff introduces case, may pre-empt off cases                                                         |
| CX     | 3 min   | Neg questions Aff                                                                                   |
| 1NC    | 8 min   | Neg introduces off cases                                                                            |
| CX     | 3 min   | Aff questions Neg                                                                                   |
| 2AC    | 8 min   | Tries to answer all off cases without dropping anything; often first speech with impact calc        |
| CX     | 3 min   | Neg questions Aff                                                                                   |
| 2NC    | 8 min   | Extends off cases/responds to what 2AC said (may drop off cases); impact calc typically starts here |
| CX     | 3 min   | Aff questions Neg                                                                                   |
| 1NR    | 5 min   | Covers off cases 2NC didn't                                                                         |
| 1AR    | 5 min   | Must answer entire neg block. Hardest speech; may preempt impact calc for the 2AR                   |
| 2NR    | 5 min   | Collapse to 1-2 args; frame the round and impact calc                                               |
| 2AR    | 5 min   | Answers 2NR, impact calc                                                                            |


**Negative block:** 2NC + 1NR = 13 consecutive minutes (HS) / 15 (college).
**Prep time:** 8 min/team (HS), 10 min/team (college).

Constructives = build arguments. Rebuttals = refute + extend. Neg gets presumption by default. Aff must prove the plan beats the status quo (squo).

## Aff Basics



### Stock Issues (used in lay debate)

- **Inherency** — problem exists, squo isn't fixing it
- **Harms** — significant negative consequences in the squo the plan addresses
- **Solvency** — plan actually fixes or reduces those harms
- **Topicality** — plan falls within the bounds of the resolution



### Advantages (used in flow/circuit debate)

- Advantage 1, 2, rarely 3. Each advantage is a reason why the plan is good, and usually ends in an impact.

## Neg Toolkit



### Disadvantages (DAs)

Plan causes harmful consequences that outweigh the advantages.

- **Uniqueness** — the harm doesn't exist (or is trending away) in the squo
- **Link** — the plan triggers the harm
- **Internal link** — the chain connecting link to impact
- **Impact** — the harm itself (magnitude, probability, timeframe)

To beat a DA: turn it, no-link it, outweigh on impact calculus, or take out uniqueness. A terminal impact is the final harm in the chain (extinction, nuclear war). The normal impact can be something smaller like inequality, nuke prolif, or democracy. It's best to have an impact external to the aff, so the aff can't just claim that passing the plan solves the DA's impacts, which makes the perm easier.

### Counterplans (CPs)

Alternative policy that solves the case without doing the plan as written.

Must be **competitive**: either mutually exclusive with the plan, or net beneficial relative to doing both (the perm).

**Net benefit:** The DA or advantage the CP solves/avoids that the plan doesn't. This is *why* the CP is a reason to reject the Aff, not just an alternative.

**Permutation (perm):** The Aff's test of CP competitiveness. Perms are essentially free arguments for the Aff. They cost almost nothing to throw in the 2AC, and the Neg has to spend time answering them.

**Types of perms:**

- **Perm Do Both (PDB)** — do the plan and the CP simultaneously; tests whether they're actually mutually exclusive
- **Perm Do the CP (PDC)** — the Aff just does the CP, arguing it's not competitive because it's functionally the plan (used vs. agent CPs, minor process shifts)
- **Perm Do the Plan and [X non-exclusive part of the CP]** — isolates the part of the CP that isn't mutually exclusive and does that plus the plan, leaving the CP with no independent net benefit
- **Delay perm** — do the plan after the CP's or DA's timeframe passes (can be applied to CPs and to timeframe-based DAs)

**Types of CPs:**

1. **Agent CP** — some other actor (states, another branch or agency) does the plan instead
2. **Process CP** — still results in the aff, but through a different process (e.g. a consult CP)
3. **Advantage CP** — everything else, such as a different mechanism that gets the advantage without being either of the above

**Conditionality (condo) vs. dispositionality (dispo):** The neg will always have alternate worlds (CPs, Ks). Condo means the neg can kick out of any of them at any point. Neg runs "condo good" (it helps neg strategy); aff runs "condo bad" (it's unfair to be forced to answer multiple worlds that may get dropped). Dispo means the neg can only kick out of an alternate world if it gets permed or the aff runs theory on it.

**Kicking a CP:** To strategically concede part or all of a CP, you can concede the perm (partly to look dispo rather than condo), or just drop it normally.

**For a CP to win**, it can't just be a good idea. It has to be better than doing the aff. The neg proves this by pairing the CP with a DA that serves as the net benefit. The impact of a CP should be an external impact.

### Kritiks (Ks)

The Aff's assumptions, discourse, or representations are flawed and should be rejected independent of or prior to the plan's policy merits.

- **Link** — how the Aff's discourse/assumptions/method triggers the K
- **Impact** — the harm from those assumptions (often framed as prior to or a precondition for policy analysis)
- **Alternative** — what the Neg advocates instead (reject the aff, different epistemology/ethic, specific praxis)
- **Framework** — why the K gets evaluated before/instead of the plan's consequences (fiat is illusory, representations matter first, etc.)

**Common K literature bases:** capitalism, biopower/Foucault, security, anthropocentrism, militarism, settler colonialism, afropessimism, disability studies, neoliberalism, necropolitics, structural racism/CRT

**K Affs:** Instead of defending a traditional USFG policy action, some Aff teams read a "K aff": a case built around a kritikal thesis, a piece of performance, or a methodology critique rather than a plan text with conventional solvency. K affs usually still have to answer whether they defend the resolution/USFG action, which becomes a framework/T-USFG debate. Teams that don't read Ks themselves should still expect to face K affs and need a framework strategy (T-USFG, fairness/education impacts, switch-side debate good) rather than object-level K answers.

### Topicality (T)

Aff's plan doesn't fall within the bounds of the resolution. This is a jurisdictional voter: reject regardless of plan merits.

- **Interpretation** — the neg's definition of the resolutional term
- **Violation** — what the Aff did wrong relative to that interpretation
- **Standards** — why your interpretation is better (limits, ground, predictability, bright-line)
- **Voting issue** — why T should be a voter (jurisdiction, fairness, education)



### Theory

Procedural fairness/education objections to how the debate itself is being conducted. Structurally identical to T but aimed at in-round practice rather than resolutional wording.

**Common shells:** conditionality bad, multiple conditional worlds bad, PICs bad, floating PIKs bad, consult CPs bad (illegitimate/artificially competitive), states CP theory (50-state fiat is unpredictable/not real-world)

Components: interpretation, violation, standards, voting issue.

**Common answers to T and theory shells:**

- **Functional limits** — their interpretation is unconstrained and forces the aff to prepare for an unpredictable range of niche arguments, killing in-depth education and fairness. Functional limits ensure both sides can have a deep, high-quality debate on core issues.
- **Side bias** — neg already has structural advantages (the block, condo), so don't vote on procedurals unless the aff is actually being unfair.
- **Arbitrariness and reasonability** — their interpretation is arbitrary and leads to a slippery slope with no logical stopping point. Reasonability says the judge shouldn't vote on minor theory violations unless real in-round abuse happened.



### Ways the Neg Can Win

- **DA + CP** — prove the plan is bad (DA) and that there's something better to do instead (CP). This is the cleanest 2NR: CP solves the aff, DA is a reason not to do the plan, perm fails.
- **Just a DA (no CP)** — if the DA outweighs the aff's advantages, the neg wins because voting neg (nothing) beats voting aff (a bad plan). You don't need an alternative to win.
- **Just a CP is not enough** — a CP alone proves there's a better option, but doesn't prove the plan is *bad*. Without a net benefit/DA, the aff can always perm or argue "do both," and the neg has no offense.
- **Case-side answers** — no-link, solvency deficits, or straight-up turning the aff's advantages can be enough on their own if you take out enough of the aff's offense that the risk of the plan doing anything good is outweighed by whatever residual neg offense exists.
- **T/Theory** — winning topicality or a theory shell is a standalone way to win regardless of the substance of the round.
- **K + Framework** — win that the aff's assumptions/discourse are bad (the K) and win the framework debate for why that comes before or outweighs the plan's policy impacts.

## How the Aff Answers Off-Case Positions

**Vs. DAs:**

- No-link (the specific plan doesn't trigger the mechanism)
- Link turn (the plan actually reduces the harm the DA describes)
- Impact turn (the DA's impact is actually good or acceptable)
- Uniqueness (the harm is already happening or inevitable absent the plan, or isn't happening at all)

**Vs. CPs:**

- Perms (see types above)
- Solvency deficits (the CP solves less of the aff's advantage than the plan)
- Theory (PICs bad, consult bad, states/50-state fiat bad, condo bad)
- Argue the CP's net benefit is also a link to the CP, or that the CP doesn't avoid the DA

**Vs. Ks:**

- Perm (do the plan and the alt: material policy change alongside a shift in framing/epistemology)
- Alt fails/no solvency (rejecting the aff doesn't materially help anyone)
- Framework (fiat/consequences are a legitimate lens; large-scale material harm outweighs purely discursive impact; policymaking education is an impact turn to their framework)
- Link turn (the plan resists the K's impact rather than feeding it)

**Vs. T:**

- We meet (the plan is inside their interpretation)
- Counter-interpretation (offer a competing definition and win it's better)
- Reasonability, plus the standards-level answers above

**Order of the 2AC:** answer T, theory, procedurals, and framework first (if you don't answer them you can lose outright), then counterplans (so you can lay out perms and intrinsic objections that dictate how the DAs interact with both plans), then DAs and Ks.

## Neg Strategy Basics

- **The block's purpose** is to use 13 minutes to overwhelm the 5-minute 1AR. A good target is about a 2:1 ratio of cards to the aff.
- **Offense before defense.** As neg, in any speech (except maybe the 2NR), lead with offensive positions (DAs, CPs, Ks, T) before defensive ones.
- **Split of the block:** The 2NC typically covers case plus one other position (often a CP, T, or K), leaving the DAs for the 1NR.
- **Run as many offs as you can in the 1NC** to overwhelm the 2AC. You can always drop positions in the block (condo).
- **Impact defense (!D)** answers the impacts the aff claims in its advantages. It applies to any argument. The aff can use impact defense against a DA's impacts, too.
- **Straight turn:** a link turn plus a no-uniqueness (NUQ) argument on the same DA. This flips the argument from "plan causes the harm" into "plan causes the good thing." Once you've straight-turned a DA, you can drop your own advantages and go for the DA, though it's uncommon.
- **Try or die:** a Pascal's wager applied to a plan's solvency. Make a 2x2 grid of {plan solves, plan doesn't solve} against {pass the plan, don't pass it}. If the impact is existential and the only way it's solved is passing the plan, then passing it dominates, since the downside of the plan failing is no worse than the squo.

## Impact Calc

Impact calculus (impact calc) is the comparative weighing of impacts across the flow. It tells the judge *why* your impact matters more than theirs, not just that it exists. It's what resolves close rounds when both sides are winning some offense.

**The three main weighing mechanisms:**

- **Magnitude** — how big is the impact (number of people/scale of harm)?
- **Probability** — how likely is the impact to actually happen given the link chain?
- **Timeframe** — how soon does the impact occur (immediate vs. long-term)?

Other common weighing mechanisms: reversibility (can the harm be undone?) and systemic vs. existential framing.

**Where impact calc happens in the round:**

- **2AC/2NC** — the block is typically the earliest point impact calc gets extended in a substantive way, once both sides have their offense on the flow
- **1AR** — sometimes preempts impact calc that the aff expects the neg to make in the 2NR, especially on args the 1AR knows will get collapsed to
- **2NR/2AR** — where impact calc is most decisive. The last two speeches should be built almost entirely around comparative weighing rather than just extending tags, since by this point the judge needs a clear framework for resolving conflicting offense

## Evidence (Cards)

**Anatomy of a card:**

- **Tag** — 1-2 sentence summary of the argument (the claim the card is supposed to prove)
- **Citation (cite)** — author, publication, date, and the author's qualifications. Only the short form (e.g. "Smith 24") is read aloud
- **Body** — the excerpted text of the source, with key lines underlined/highlighted

**What gets read:**

- Underlined/highlighted = what gets read aloud. The rest is skipped context.
- The tag is read in full, even though it's typically not highlighted (it's its own heading style, not card body text).
- Debaters usually say "next" before the tag to signal a new card, and "that's" before the author. You can skip "that's" to save time.

**What opponents and judges can do:**

- Opponents can take and examine cards even during your speech.
- Judges can "call for cards" after the round to evaluate contested evidence.

**Spreading (speed reading):** extremely fast delivery, hundreds of words per minute, to fit more arguments. It's the national circuit standard. Know your judge.

### How Speech Docs Are Read Aloud

- **Highlighted text is what actually gets read aloud.** Underlining is not spoken. It marks the range that's important enough to potentially extend into, but only the highlighted portion is read in the speech itself.
- **Taglines are read in full**, even though they're typically not highlighted or underlined. They're their own heading style.
- **The cite (author last name + date) is read aloud**, e.g. "Smith '24". This is the short form, not the full author/qualifications/publication line.
- **Small, non-highlighted text within a card is still part of the evidence.** It isn't spoken, but it's fair game for the other team to read, prep out, or call out if it undercuts the highlighted portion (e.g. a caveat or contradicting warrant sitting in the un-highlighted body).
- **Bolding is a visual aid, not a read/skip signal.** It's typically used to help the speaker's eye find key phrases within the highlighted text while spreading, not to mark separate content.

## Flowing

Column-based note system, one column per speech, with arguments tracked horizontally across the round. Each off-case argument (DA, CP, K, T) gets its own separate flow sheet. Use shorthand and symbols.

## Verbatim & File Conventions

Debate files are usually built in MS Word with the **Verbatim** plugin (formatting via the Debate ribbon), or in **CardMirror**, a debate-specific editor that does the formatting without needing Word. Never apply formatting directly to text. Modify the styles themselves instead (Verbatim > Settings > Styles).

Key Verbatim styles:

- **Analytic** — for written blocks/analytics; stripped from the send document automatically
- **Undertag** — for notes on evidence; also stripped from the send doc and doesn't appear in the nav pane

**Header naming rules:**

1. Use **AT** for "Answers To," never "A2."
2. Don't over-abbreviate headers. Different people use different schemes, and abbreviating breaks clarity mid-round.
3. Backload the speech name (put it at the end, not the front). What the card does matters more than when it's read.
4. Extensions or non-essential cards get their own header, marked **Ext**, so it's clear which arguments are must-make vs. supplementary.
5. The header separator is `---` (triple dash).

Example headers:

```
Aff: China War Good---2AC
Link---AT: Royalties---1AR
Perm---Do CP---AT: Peake

Neg: Solvency---Standing---AT: Goldwater---2NC
U---2NC
Link---ICBMs---2NC
Perm---AT: Other Issues---Ext---Ripeness
```

## Key Terms


| Term                   | Meaning                                                                                                 |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Uniqueness (UQ)        | Squo doesn't already cause the harm                                                                     |
| Link                   | How the plan triggers the impact                                                                        |
| Internal link (IL)     | Chain connecting link to impact                                                                         |
| Impact                 | The bad thing that happens                                                                              |
| Terminal impact        | The final harm in a chain, where "so what?" has no further answer                                       |
| Solvency               | Does the plan actually fix the problem?                                                                 |
| Solvency deficit       | CP solves less of the aff's advantage than the plan; the unsolved leftover                              |
| Perm                   | Aff's test of CP competitiveness. PDB = perm do both, PDC = perm do the counterplan                     |
| Net benefit            | The DA or advantage a CP solves/avoids that the plan doesn't. The reason to prefer the CP over the perm |
| Turn                   | Their argument actually helps you                                                                       |
| Conditionality (condo) | Neg can drop args without penalty (controversial)                                                       |
| Dispo                  | Neg can only kick an alternate world if it gets permed or the aff runs theory                           |
| Going for              | Collapsing to 1-2 args in late rebuttals                                                                |
| Spreading              | Speaking very fast to get more args in                                                                  |
| Flowing                | Note-taking system, columns per speech, one flow per arg                                                |
| Card                   | A piece of evidence (tag + citation + body)                                                             |
| Tag                    | 1-2 sentence summary of what a card says                                                                |
| Drop                   | Failing to answer an argument (usually = losing it)                                                     |
| Extend                 | Carrying an argument forward through rebuttals                                                          |
| Weighing               | Comparing your impacts to theirs (magnitude, probability, timeframe)                                    |
| AT                     | "Answers To." Correct shorthand (never "A2")                                                            |
| Durable fiat           | Plan stays in effect for the round, not just passes; blocks "it gets repealed later" args               |
| Verbatim               | Microsoft Word add-on for formatting debate cards                                                       |
| CardMirror             | Standalone debate editor that does Verbatim-style formatting without Word                               |
| !D                     | File shorthand for Impact Defense (the ! stands for impact), so !T would mean impact turn               |
